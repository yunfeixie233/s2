The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH)The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al, 2024) argues that as vision and language models scale, their representation spaces converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. The reason is that images and text are both projections of the same underlying world, so better models of either one should end up organizing that world in similar ways.

In this work, we bring PRH to robotics and ask: can physical actions be viewed as another projection of that world, such that more capable embodied models better align their vision and language representations with the structure of motion trajectories?

Our evidence suggests the answer is yes. To test this, we measure how well an embodied model's representation of a sensory input (what it sees and what it is told) aligns with the motor trajectory that should follow. Put simply, if two situations look alike to the model, they should also call for similar motions. We call this measure sensorimotor alignment (S²).

Our first finding is that S² is positively related to downstream robot task success. We test a range of Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) and World Action Models (WAMs), both in simulation and on real robots. In every setting, models with higher S² achieve higher success rates. This suggests that PRH also holds in robotics: a more capable embodied model is one whose vision and language representations better reflect the structure of physical motion.

S² also explains, at the representation level, several behaviors that prior work has only observed empirically. For example, VLAs often ignore the language instruction and act mainly on what they see. We find that their success tracks the S² of their vision features, not their text features.

In practice, S² can serve as a low-cost offline proxy for identifying which models are likely to achieve higher task success before closed-loop evaluation. For instance, it can identify promising VLM backbones even before costly VLA fine-tuning.

Measuring sensorimotor alignment

Camera features and robot trajectories live in different spaces, so we cannot compare them directly. Following PRH,The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al, 2024) we instead compare their similarity structures: does each representation judge the same pairs of episodes to be alike? Each side gives us a kernel KK, a matrix of pairwise similarities over the same episodes, and S² scores how well the two kernels agree.

We compute S² on a fixed offline dataset 𝒟={ei}i=1N\mathcal{D} = \{e_i\}_{i=1}^N, such as DROID.DROID: A Large-Scale In-the-Wild Robot Manipulation Dataset (Khazatsky et al, 2024) Each episode ei=(𝐱i,𝛕i)e_i = (\mathbf{x}_i, \boldsymbol{\tau}_i) pairs a sensory input 𝐱i\mathbf{x}_i, made up of a camera image and a language instruction, with the trajectory 𝛕i\boldsymbol{\tau}_i that a human teleoperator then executed. Every model is scored against the same recorded motions, so no closed-loop rollouts are needed.

The sensory kernel comes from the model. We feed 𝐱i\mathbf{x}_i to the model, extract a feature vector 𝐳i\mathbf{z}_i, and compare episodes by cosine similarity, Kijsensory=cos⁡(𝐳i,𝐳j)K^{\mathrm{sensory}}_{ij} = \cos(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_j). For video datasets, we use only the first frame of each episode, matching standard VLA inputs. Because this works for any architecture and layer, we can compare vision features, text features, or features taken at different depths.

The motor kernel comes from the robot. Each trajectory records the end-effector position, orientation, and gripper state, expressed in the robot base frame. The same task can be performed faster or slower, so we compare trajectories with dynamic time warping (DTW),Dynamic Programming Algorithm Optimization for Spoken Word Recognition (Sakoe and Chiba, 1978) which aligns their timing before measuring distance, Kijmotor=DTW(𝛕i,𝛕j)K^{\mathrm{motor}}_{ij} = \mathrm{DTW}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_i, \boldsymbol{\tau}_j). The local DTW cost uses geodesic distance on SO(3) for rotations and native coordinates for positions and gripper state.

To compare the two kernels, we look at neighborhoods. Take one episode as the query and find its kk nearest neighbors under each kernel. The illustration below uses k=3k = 3; our experiments use k=10k = 10. If the model organizes episodes by the motion they require, the two neighbor sets should largely overlap.

S² aggregates this agreement over every query episode. We use Centered Kernel Nearest-Neighbor Alignment (CKNNA) from PRH,The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al, 2024) which measures how strongly the two kernels co-vary within these shared neighborhoods:

S2=CKNNA(Ksensory,Kmotor)\mathrm{S}^2 = \mathrm{CKNNA}\left(K^{\mathrm{sensory}}, K^{\mathrm{motor}}\right)

A high S² means the model places episodes close together when they call for similar motions, not merely when they look or sound alike.

S² Relates to Robot Task Success Rate

Action is a harder test case for PRHThe Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al, 2024) than vision or language: it is a physical trajectory, not another passive observation. We test a concrete prediction: a model's S² should positively relate to its downstream robot task success. If it does, S² can serve as a low-cost offline proxy for identifying which models are likely to achieve higher task success before closed-loop evaluation.

Below, we show this relation across three model families and real-robot policies, compare S² with VQA and video-quality metrics, and check that it is robust to evaluation choices.

S² Relates to Task Success Across Model Families

We ask whether stronger alignment between sensory and motor similarity structures accompanies higher task success. In the figure below, we test this across three model families and on policies evaluated on real robots. For each model, we compute S² from its sensory features on a reference dataset (x-axis) and compare it with the model's downstream manipulation success rate (y-axis). Appendix A.4 of our paper lists the evaluated VLAs, VLMs, WAMs, and real-robot policies, along with their evaluation protocols and conditions.

Vision-language-action models (VLAs). We test 12 VLAs against their SimplerEnvEvaluating Real-World Robot Manipulation Policies in Simulation (Li et al, 2024) success rates. The correlation r = +0.717 is strong. This supports our premise that S² captures a control-relevant representation.

Vision-language models (VLMs). We ask whether S² computed on a raw VLM, before any robot fine-tuning, relates to the success of the VLA built from it. We compute S² for 8 raw VLM backbones from VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026) on the DROID datasetDROID: A Large-Scale In-the-Wild Robot Manipulation Dataset (Khazatsky et al, 2024) and compare it with the SimplerEnvEvaluating Real-World Robot Manipulation Policies in Simulation (Li et al, 2024) success of the VLAs that VLM4VLA fine-tunes from them. We find a strong correlation with downstream VLA success: r = +0.681. This result demonstrates the practical utility of S²: models with stronger sensorimotor alignment before fine-tuning tend to achieve higher downstream task success, so S² can identify promising VLM backbones even before costly VLA fine-tuning.

World action models (WAMs). Finally, we ask whether the relation extends to WAMs, which build on video generators rather than VLMs. We compute S² for 6 WAMs on the bimanual ALOHA datasetLearning Fine-Grained Bimanual Manipulation with Low-Cost Hardware (Zhao et al, 2023) and compare it with their success on RoboTwin 2.0RoboTwin: Dual-Arm Robot Benchmark with Generative Digital Twins (Mu et al, 2025). For WAMs, the sensory representation is the video-generator latent used for action decoding, and all 6 models share the same ALOHA trajectories and S² settings. Across these 6 models, S² shows a strong correlation with success, r = +0.718.

Real-robot policies. We test whether the relation between S² and task success in simulation extends to real robots. Because real-world success depends on the task set, robot, cameras, and protocol, we use the physical-robot success rates that MolmoB0TMolmoB0T: Large-Scale Simulation Enables Zero-Shot Manipulation (Deshpande et al, 2026) reports for 5 policies on the same 40 tasks, and compute S² for their public checkpoints on the same 6,000-episode DROID subset. Across these 5 policies, S² shows a strong correlation with real-robot task success, r = +0.831.

We further validate that S² is related to downstream task success for frozen vision backbones under low-data behavior cloning and visual-reward learning (Appendix B.1 of our paper).

S² Relates to Task Success More Closely Than Alternative Metrics

Prior work asks whether offline evaluation scores predict downstream control performance. VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026) examines the correlation between VQA benchmark scores and the performance of the resulting VLA policies, finding weak or even negative correlations. For video models, Tian et al.A Control-Centric Benchmark for Video Prediction (Tian et al, 2023) compare the quality of WAM-generated video with downstream control performance and find that the agreement is inconsistent across tasks. A common limitation is that these metrics measure language understanding or pixel-level fidelity, neither of which is grounded in the physical actions the robot must execute. S² addresses this gap by directly linking perception to action. As we show below, S² correlates more strongly with task success rate than any VQA or video-quality metric we evaluate.

S² correlates with VLA success where VQA scores do not. We compare S² with aggregate VQA scores as offline proxies of downstream VLA success, using the same 8 raw VLM backbones and evaluation setting as in the VLM comparison above. We find that VQA scores show virtually no correlation with VLA success at r = −0.154 (figure below, right), whereas S² correlates strongly at r = +0.681 (figure below, left). S² thus tracks VLA success where VQA benchmarks do not.

S² correlates with WAM success more closely than video-quality metrics. We compare S² with standard video-quality metrics (FIDGANs Trained by a Two Time-Scale Update Rule Converge to a Local Nash Equilibrium (Heusel et al, 2017), SSIMImage Quality Assessment: From Error Visibility to Structural Similarity (Wang et al, 2004), and PSNRImage Quality Metrics: PSNR vs. SSIM (Horé and Ziou, 2010)) on 3 of the WAMs, MotusMotus: A Unified Latent Action World Model (Bi et al, 2026), LingBot-VACausal World Modeling for Robot Control (Li et al, 2026), and VidarVidar: Embodied Video Diffusion Model for Generalist Manipulation (Feng et al, 2025), evaluated on RoboTwin. We find that video-quality metrics show no significant correlations with task success: SSIM reaches only r = +0.558 and PSNR only r = +0.468 (figure below). FID even correlates in the wrong direction. Because lower FID indicates higher video quality, a useful FID proxy would correlate negatively with success; instead, we find r = +0.680. In contrast, S² computed on ALOHA episodes correlates far more strongly at r = +0.999 and is the only metric that correctly recovers the success-rate ordering. Pixel-level prediction quality thus shows little relation to the control success that S² captures.

S² Is Robust to Evaluation Choices

A useful offline metric should satisfy two complementary properties: stability to nuisance choices in how it is computed and sensitivity to physically meaningful mismatches between the reference data and the downstream task. We vary 9 factors in evaluating S² and find that the correlation with success stays positive under all 8 nuisance choices and collapses only when the reference data have the wrong arm configuration. We present 2 nuisance factors below. In our paper, Appendix C.1 tests the other six, Appendix C.3 details the arm-configuration collapse, and Appendix E reports per-model S² values.

The correlation transfers across reference datasets. We validate that S² is not tied to a particular reference dataset. We compute S² on both DROID (Franka Panda) and BridgeDataV2BridgeData V2: A Dataset for Robot Learning at Scale (Walke et al, 2023) (WidowX), two datasets with different arm kinematics. Both yield strong correlations near r = +0.7 with downstream success for the 12 VLAs on SimplerEnv (figure below), confirming that S² captures a transferable property of the representation rather than a dataset-specific artifact.

The correlation is stable across trajectory horizons. We validate that S² is not sensitive to the trajectory horizon h used during evaluation. Across both reference datasets, the correlation remains above r = 0.5 from short horizons through h = 220 (figure below), confirming that S² reflects a persistent structural property rather than an artifact of a particular horizon choice.

Representation-Level Explanations with S²

Prior work reports several phenomena in VLAs and WAMs based only on closed-loop results. Because S² relates to task success, as shown above, we use it to provide a representation-level explanation for each of these phenomena, as summarized below:

  • Modality dominance: prior work observes that VLAs often struggle with instruction groundingLIBERO-Plus: A Progressive Robustness Benchmark for Visual-Language-Action Models (Fei et al, 2026)When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs (Fang et al, 2026)Restoring Linguistic Grounding in VLA Models via Train-Free Attention Recalibration (Zhang et al, 2026). We explain that after VLA fine-tuning, task success is associated with vision-feature S² but not text-feature S², indicating that fine-tuned VLAs rely mainly on visual rather than language representations for control.
  • Frozen encoders: prior work observes that freezing the vision encoder during VLA fine-tuning degrades downstream task performanceVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026). We explain that freezing also prevents the S² gain from end-to-end fine-tuning, indicating that fine-tuning improves control by aligning the visual representation more closely with motor structure.
  • Iterative denoising: prior work observes that WAMs can skip test-time video generation with little loss in task successFast-WAM: Do World Action Models Need Test-time Future Imagination? (Yuan et al, 2026). We explain that S² is already high at the earliest denoising step and changes little afterwards, indicating that the sensorimotor structure needed for control is present before denoising finishes.
  • Mid-depth blocks: prior work observes that mid-depth blocks of video diffusion transformers are especially important for semantic reasoningDemystifying Video Reasoning (Wang et al, 2026). We explain that the same mid-depth blocks carry the strongest S², indicating that the layers that reason about video semantics are also the layers most aligned with motor structure.

What Carries the Correlation: Vision, Text, or Both?

Prior work observes that VLAs often struggle with instruction grounding, acting on what the scene suggests rather than on what the instruction asksLIBERO-Plus: A Progressive Robustness Benchmark for Visual-Language-Action Models (Fei et al, 2026)When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs (Fang et al, 2026)Restoring Linguistic Grounding in VLA Models via Train-Free Attention Recalibration (Zhang et al, 2026). We ask which modality carries the correlation with task success: vision, text, or both. In the figure below, we compute S² separately for each modality. We take the VLM output tokens read by the action head and form vision features from the image tokens, text features from the instruction tokens, and joint vision-text features from all tokens. The full per-model S² values across all feature positions are reported in Appendix D.1 of our paper.

Fine-tuned VLAs rely mainly on visual rather than language representations for control. At horizon h = 3, we find that S² from vision features correlates with the SimplerEnv success of the 12 fine-tuned VLAs at r = +0.694, nearly matching joint vision-text features at r = +0.699, while S² from text features shows no significant relation at r = +0.237. Before fine-tuning, the pattern differs: in raw VLMs, S² from text features correlates at r = +0.501, more strongly than vision features at r = +0.425, and joint vision-text features correlate most strongly at r = +0.681.

We therefore infer that fine-tuned VLAs rely mainly on visual rather than language representations for control, which explains their weak instruction grounding at the representation level.

Freezing Vision Encoders Limits S² Gain

VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026) reports that freezing the vision encoder during VLA fine-tuning severely degrades downstream robotics performance. We use S² to test whether end-to-end fine-tuning improves control by increasing sensorimotor alignment in the action-head input.

In the figure below, we compare 2 VLAs differing only in whether the vision encoder is frozen during fine-tuning. Both attach an FCDecoder action head to Qwen2.5-VL-3BQwen2.5-VL Technical Report (Bai et al, 2025) and are fine-tuned on BridgeDataV2 with the same recipe, following VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026). For each, we compute S² from the joint vision-text features read by the action head and measure SimplerEnv success. Freezing the encoder reduces S² by 41% and downstream success by 40%. The S² drop holds at all 10 motion horizons, ranging from 38% to 49% (Appendix D.2 of our paper).

We infer that end-to-end fine-tuning improves control by aligning visual representations with motor structure, which explains why freezing the vision encoder lowers task success.

Early-Denoising Features Already Carry Strong Sensorimotor Alignment

WAMs typically decode actions from video latents obtained through iterative denoising. Fast-WAMFast-WAM: Do World Action Models Need Test-time Future Imagination? (Yuan et al, 2026) shows that this process can be skipped at inference with little loss in task success. We use S² to test whether sensorimotor alignment is already present at early denoising steps.

In the figure below, we track S² along the denoising schedule of 3 WAMsMotus: A Unified Latent Action World Model (Bi et al, 2026)Causal World Modeling for Robot Control (Li et al, 2026)Vidar: Embodied Video Diffusion Model for Generalist Manipulation (Feng et al, 2025) on RoboTwinRoboTwin: Dual-Arm Robot Benchmark with Generative Digital Twins (Mu et al, 2025). For each model, we extract latent features and compute S² at 5 points in its native schedule, from 0% to 100%. S² is already high at the earliest point and remains nearly flat, varying by at most 6% from its initial value across all 3 models. We therefore infer that the sensorimotor structure needed for control is present before denoising finishes. Because S² correlates with WAM success, as shown above, this explains why skipping iterative video denoising barely lowers task success.

Mid-Depth Blocks Carry the Strongest Sensorimotor Alignment

Prior analysis of Chain-of-StepsDemystifying Video Reasoning (Wang et al, 2026) shows that mid-depth layers in Video Diffusion Transformers are especially important for semantic reasoning over objects and interactions. We test whether the strongest sensorimotor signal for embodied control also lies within this semantic window.

In the figure below, we compute S² at different depths of 3 WAMsMotus: A Unified Latent Action World Model (Bi et al, 2026)Causal World Modeling for Robot Control (Li et al, 2026)Vidar: Embodied Video Diffusion Model for Generalist Manipulation (Feng et al, 2025) on RoboTwin. All 3 share a 30-block video backbone, and we extract features from Blocks 0, 7, 14, 21, and 29. Block 14 consistently yields the highest S² across all 3 models. We find that this peak falls within the mid-depth semantic window identified by Chain-of-StepsDemystifying Video Reasoning (Wang et al, 2026), and infer that the layers that reason about video semantics are also the layers most aligned with motor structure. Appendix D.3 of our paper gives the full analysis.
