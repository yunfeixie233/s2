The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (PRH)The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al, 2024) argues that as vision and language models scale, their representation spaces converge toward a shared statistical model of reality. The idea is that images and text are different projections of the same underlying world. As models recover that world more faithfully, they begin to agree on which inputs are similar. For example, if a vision model places two images close together in the representation space, a language model tends to place their captions close together too.

In this work, we bring PRH to robotics and ask: can physical motion be viewed as another projection of that world, such that more capable embodied models better align their vision and language representations with the structure of motion trajectories?

Our evidence suggests the answer is yes. To test this, we use Sensorimotor Alignment (S²), which measures the overlap between neighborhoods of a model's sensory representations and neighborhoods of end-effector trajectories. Higher alignment means episodes nearby in sensory representation space are also nearby in motor representation space.

Our first finding is that S² is positively related to downstream robot task success. We test a range of Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) and World Action Models (WAMs) in simulation, as well as policies on real robots. Across these settings, models with higher S² tend to achieve higher task success rates. This pattern provides empirical support for extending PRH to robotics.

S² also explains, at the representation level, several phenomena that prior work has observed only through closed-loop results. For example, VLAs often ignore the language instruction and act mainly on what they see. We find that their task success correlates with the S² of their vision features, not their text features.

In practice, S² can serve as a low-cost offline proxy for identifying which models are likely to achieve higher task success before closed-loop evaluation. For instance, it can identify promising vision-language model (VLM) backbones even before costly VLA fine-tuning.

Measuring sensorimotor alignment#

For each recorded episode, the model's image and text features form its sensory representation, and the end-effector trajectory forms its motor representation. We compute S² by constructing these representations and comparing their neighborhoods, as follows.

Reference dataset. To measure cross-modal alignment, we use the DROIDDROID: A Large-Scale In-the-Wild Robot Manipulation Dataset (Khazatsky et al, 2024) and BridgeDataV2 datasets, which pair sensory inputs with physical motions. From a dataset, we sample NN episodes and compute both representations from each episode. For episode ii, we randomly sample one image frame and pair it with the task instruction. This image-text pair forms the sensory input 𝐱i\mathbf{x}_i.

Sensory representation. For each episode ii, we encode the image frame and text instruction with the vision and text encoders of a robot foundation model, obtaining a sensory embedding 𝐳i=f(𝐱i)∈ℝd\mathbf{z}_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}. Here, ff applies the vision encoder to the image and the text encoder to the instruction, and we average-pool the output token features into a single dd-dimensional vector.

Motor representation. For the sampled image frame in 𝐱i\mathbf{x}_i, we extract end-effector positions, orientations, and gripper states over the next hh steps, forming the motor representation 𝛕i∈ℝh×7\boldsymbol{\tau}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times 7}. Each row contains the position coordinates x,y,zx,y,z, roll, pitch, and yaw angles ϕ,θ,ψ\phi,\theta,\psi, and the recorded gripper coordinate gg. We encode positions as vectors in ℝ3\mathbb{R}^3 and orientation angles as rotations in SO(3), so that distances reflect the geometry of translations and rotations.

Sensorimotor alignment. We measure alignment with a mutual nearest-neighbor metric comparing the kk-nearest-neighbor sets in sensory and motor spaces. Greater overlap indicates stronger alignment between an episode's sensory and motor representations. To identify these neighbors, we use cosine distance in sensory space and dynamic time warping (DTW)Dynamic Programming Algorithm Optimization for Spoken Word Recognition (Sakoe and Chiba, 1978) in motor space to compare trajectories executed at different speeds. Smaller distances mean closer neighbors:

dsensory(i,j)=1−cos⁡(𝐳i,𝐳j),d_{\mathrm{sensory}}(i,j) = 1 - \cos(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_j),dmotor(i,j)=DTW(𝛕i,𝛕j)d_{\mathrm{motor}}(i,j) = \mathrm{DTW}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_i, \boldsymbol{\tau}_j)

For each episode ii, we use dsensoryd_{\mathrm{sensory}} to form the sensory kk-nearest-neighbor set 𝒩sensory(i)\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{sensory}}(i) and dmotord_{\mathrm{motor}} to form the motor kk-nearest-neighbor set 𝒩motor(i)\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{motor}}(i). We then take the intersection of the two sets, divide its size by kk, and average over all NN episodes:The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al, 2024)

mNN=1Nk∑i=1N|𝒩sensory(i)∩𝒩motor(i)|m_{\mathrm{NN}} = \frac{1}{Nk}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{sensory}}(i)\cap\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{motor}}(i)\right|

Greater overlap indicates that episodes with similar sensory representations also have similar motor trajectories, as the figure below shows for a single query episode.

Of the four sensory neighbors shown, two are shared with the motor neighbor set (green checks). The other two are found only in the sensory set (red crosses). One of them even shares the query's instruction, "place cans into cabinet", yet calls for a different motion.

The overlap above illustrates the idea behind S². Our reported score uses a centered, normalized variant of Centered Kernel Nearest-Neighbor Alignment (CKNNA) from PRH,The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh et al, 2024) with k=10k = 10 neighbors; Appendix A.3 of our paper gives the full definition.

S² Correlates with Robot Task Success Rate#

We test our PRH-inspired prediction that models whose sensory representations align more closely with robot trajectories achieve higher task success. For each model, we compare its offline S² with independently measured closed-loop task success. A positive correlation would support both this view of embodiment and the use of S² to identify promising models before closed-loop evaluation.

Below, we establish this positive correlation across three model families and real-robot policies, and show that S² correlates with task success more closely than visual question answering (VQA) scores and video-quality metrics.

S² Correlates with Task Success Across Model Families#

The figure below compares three model families and a separate set of policies evaluated on real robots. Each panel plots S² computed on a reference dataset (x-axis) against downstream manipulation success rate (y-axis). Appendix B of our paper lists the evaluated VLAs, VLMs, world action models (WAMs), and real-robot policies, along with their evaluation protocols and conditions.

Vision-language-action models (VLAs). We test 12 VLAs against their SimplerEnvEvaluating Real-World Robot Manipulation Policies in Simulation (Li et al, 2024) success rates. The Pearson correlation r = +0.717 is strong. Thus, models with higher sensorimotor alignment tend to achieve higher task success rates.

Vision-language models (VLMs). We ask whether S² computed on a raw VLM, before any robot fine-tuning, correlates with the task success of the VLA that is later fine-tuned from that backbone. We compute S² for 8 raw VLM backbones from VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026) and compare it with the SimplerEnvEvaluating Real-World Robot Manipulation Policies in Simulation (Li et al, 2024) success rate of the VLAs that VLM4VLA fine-tunes from them. We find a strong correlation with downstream VLA success rate: r = +0.681. S² can therefore identify promising VLM backbones even before costly VLA fine-tuning.

World action models (WAMs). Finally, we test WAMs, which build on video generators rather than VLMs. We compute S² for 6 WAMs on the bimanual ALOHA datasetLearning Fine-Grained Bimanual Manipulation with Low-Cost Hardware (Zhao et al, 2023) and compare it with their success rates on RoboTwin 2.0RoboTwin: Dual-Arm Robot Benchmark with Generative Digital Twins (Mu et al, 2025). For WAMs, the sensory representation is the video-generator latent used for action decoding, and all 6 models share the same ALOHA trajectories and S² settings. Across these 6 models, S² shows a strong correlation with task success, r = +0.718.

Real-robot policies. We test whether the correlation observed in simulation extends to real robots. Because real-world success rates depend on many factors, such as the task set, robot, cameras, and protocol, we take all success rates from a single unified setting: the physical-robot results that MolmoB0TMolmoB0T: Large-Scale Simulation Enables Zero-Shot Manipulation (Deshpande et al, 2026) reports for 5 policies on 40 tasks. We then compute S² for their public checkpoints on the same DROID reference dataset. Across these 5 policies, S² shows a strong correlation with real-robot task success, r = +0.831.

We further validate that S² correlates with downstream task success for frozen vision backbones under low-data behavior cloning and visual-reward learning, as shown in Appendix B.6 of our paper.

S² Correlates with Task Success More Closely Than Alternative Metrics#

Prior work asks whether offline scores correlate with downstream task performance. VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026) examines the correlation between VQA benchmark scores and the performance of the resulting VLA policies, finding weak or even negative correlations. For video models, Tian et al.A Control-Centric Benchmark for Video Prediction (Tian et al, 2023) find that the quality of predicted video agrees inconsistently with downstream performance across tasks. A common limitation is that these metrics measure language understanding or pixel-level video quality, neither of which depends on the motion the robot must execute. S² instead compares the nearest-neighbor sets of sensory embeddings and motor trajectories. As we show below, S² correlates more strongly with task success rate than any VQA or video-quality metric we evaluate.

S² correlates with VLA success rate where VQA scores do not. We compare S² with aggregate VQA scores as offline proxies, using the same 8 raw VLM backbones and evaluation setting as in the VLM comparison above. We find that VQA scores show virtually no correlation with VLA success rate, at r = −0.154 (figure below, right). S², by contrast, correlates strongly, at r = +0.681 (figure below, left).

S² correlates with WAM success rate more closely than video-quality metrics. We compare S² with standard video-quality metrics (FIDGANs Trained by a Two Time-Scale Update Rule Converge to a Local Nash Equilibrium (Heusel et al, 2017), SSIMImage Quality Assessment: From Error Visibility to Structural Similarity (Wang et al, 2004), and PSNRImage Quality Metrics: PSNR vs. SSIM (Horé and Ziou, 2010)) on 3 of the WAMs, MotusMotus: A Unified Latent Action World Model (Bi et al, 2026), LingBot-VACausal World Modeling for Robot Control (Li et al, 2026), and VidarVidar: Embodied Video Diffusion Model for Generalist Manipulation (Feng et al, 2025), evaluated on RoboTwin. We find that video-quality metrics correlate weakly with task success: SSIM reaches only r = +0.558 and PSNR only r = +0.468, while FID even runs backwards, at r = +0.680, where lower video quality goes with higher success (figure below). By contrast, S² on ALOHA correlates more strongly, at r = +0.999.

S² Is Robust to Evaluation Choices#

We check that the correlation does not depend on a particular choice of settings, in two ways. First, when we vary how S² is computed, including the reference dataset, the trajectory horizon, and the metric implementation, the positive correlation with task success holds. Second, as a negative control, we compute S² on reference data from a mismatched embodiment, where a sound metric should lose its correlation with task success, and the correlation drops as expected. Section 3.3 and Appendix C of our paper give the full results.

Representation-Level Explanations with S²#

Several phenomena reported for VLAs and WAMs rest only on closed-loop results and still lack a representation-level explanation. Because S² correlates with task success, as shown above, we use it to explain each of these phenomena, as summarized below:

  • Modality dominance: prior work observes that VLAs often struggle with instruction grounding: they act on what the scene visually suggests rather than on what the language instruction asksLIBERO-Plus: A Progressive Robustness Benchmark for Visual-Language-Action Models (Fei et al, 2026)When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs (Fang et al, 2026)Restoring Linguistic Grounding in VLA Models via Train-Free Attention Recalibration (Zhang et al, 2026). We explain that after VLA fine-tuning, task success is associated with vision-feature S² but not text-feature S², indicating that fine-tuned VLAs draw on what they see, not on the instruction, to produce their motion.
  • Frozen encoders: prior work observes that freezing the vision encoder during VLA fine-tuning degrades downstream task performanceVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026). We explain that freezing also prevents the S² gain from end-to-end fine-tuning, indicating that VLAs gain performance by bringing their sensory representation closer to the motor representation.
  • Iterative denoising: prior work observes that WAMs can skip iterative video denoising at test time, decoding actions without generating future video, with little loss in task successFast-WAM: Do World Action Models Need Test-time Future Imagination? (Yuan et al, 2026). We explain that the S² of WAM features is already high at the earliest denoising step and changes little afterwards, indicating that the first denoising steps already yield features aligned closely enough with robot trajectories to act on.
  • Mid-depth blocks: prior work observes that mid-depth blocks of video diffusion transformers matter most for semantic reasoningDemystifying Video Reasoning (Wang et al, 2026). We explain that these blocks also carry the strongest S², indicating that the layers that reason about video semantics are also the layers most aligned with robot motion.

What Carries the Correlation: Vision, Text, or Both?#

VLAs often struggle with instruction groundingLIBERO-Plus: A Progressive Robustness Benchmark for Visual-Language-Action Models (Fei et al, 2026)When Vision Overrides Language: Evaluating and Mitigating Counterfactual Failures in VLAs (Fang et al, 2026)Restoring Linguistic Grounding in VLA Models via Train-Free Attention Recalibration (Zhang et al, 2026). We use S² to explain this at the representation level, asking which modality the correlation with task success depends on: vision, text, or both. In the figure below, we compute S² separately for each modality. We take the VLM output tokens read by the action head and form vision features from the image tokens, text features from the instruction tokens, and joint vision-text features from all tokens.

Fine-tuned VLAs rely mainly on visual rather than language representations. In the 12 fine-tuned VLAs, S² from vision features correlates with SimplerEnv success rate at r = +0.694, nearly matching the joint vision-text features at r = +0.699, whereas text features show no significant correlation at r = +0.237. The raw VLMs reverse this pattern: before fine-tuning, text features correlate at r = +0.501, more strongly than vision features at r = +0.425, and joint vision-text features correlate most strongly at r = +0.681.

We infer that, after fine-tuning, VLAs produce their motion mainly from visual rather than language representations, which explains their weak instruction grounding at the representation level. Appendix D.1 of our paper reports per-model S² at all feature positions.

Freezing Vision Encoders Limits S² Gain#

VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026) reports that VLAs perform substantially worse when their vision encoder stays frozen during fine-tuning. We use S² to explain this drop at the representation level, testing whether freezing also prevents the sensorimotor-alignment gain from end-to-end fine-tuning.

In the figure below, we compare 2 VLAs that follow the same VLM4VLAVLM4VLA: Revisiting Vision-Language-Models in Vision-Language-Action Models (Zhang et al, 2026) recipe and differ only in whether the vision encoder is trained or kept frozen. For each, we compute S² and measure SimplerEnv success rate. Freezing the encoder reduces S² by 41% and the downstream task success rate by 40%, with more detail in Appendix D.2 of our paper. These results suggest that end-to-end fine-tuning improves performance by aligning the sensory representation more closely with the motor representation.

Features at Early Denoising Steps Already Carry Strong Sensorimotor Alignment#

Most WAMs generate future video through iterative denoising and decode actions from the resulting latents. Fast-WAMFast-WAM: Do World Action Models Need Test-time Future Imagination? (Yuan et al, 2026) shows that WAMs can skip this video generation and decode actions directly, with little loss in task success. We examine the representation-level reason: whether features at the earliest denoising steps already align with robot trajectories.

We measure S² for 3 WAMsMotus: A Unified Latent Action World Model (Bi et al, 2026)Causal World Modeling for Robot Control (Li et al, 2026)Vidar: Embodied Video Diffusion Model for Generalist Manipulation (Feng et al, 2025) on RoboTwinRoboTwin: Dual-Arm Robot Benchmark with Generative Digital Twins (Mu et al, 2025), as shown in the figure below. We compute S² from each model's latent features at 5 positions in its native denoising schedule, from 0% to 100%, not at matched noise levels. S² is already high at the earliest step and remains nearly flat, varying by about 6% at most from its initial value across the 3 models. We infer that early-step features are already sufficiently aligned with robot trajectories to generate actions. Because S² correlates with WAM success rate, as shown above, this result explains why skipping iterative video denoising barely lowers task success.

Mid-Depth Blocks Carry the Strongest Sensorimotor Alignment#

Prior analysis of Chain-of-StepsDemystifying Video Reasoning (Wang et al, 2026) shows that mid-depth layers in Video Diffusion Transformers are especially important for semantic reasoning over objects and interactions. We ask whether the layers with the highest S² also lie in this mid-depth range.

In the figure below, we compute S² at different depths of 3 WAMsMotus: A Unified Latent Action World Model (Bi et al, 2026)Causal World Modeling for Robot Control (Li et al, 2026)Vidar: Embodied Video Diffusion Model for Generalist Manipulation (Feng et al, 2025) on RoboTwin. All 3 share a 30-block video backbone, and we extract features from Blocks 0, 7, 14, 21, and 29. Block 14 yields the highest S² in every model. This peak falls in the mid-depth range that Chain-of-StepsDemystifying Video Reasoning (Wang et al, 2026) identifies as semantically important, suggesting that the layers handling video semantic reasoning align most closely with robot motion. Details are in Appendix D.3 of our paper.

Conclusion#

Our results support a PRH-inspired view of embodiment: more capable models tend to align their sensory representations more closely with robot motion. S² makes this relationship measurable offline and helps identify promising models and VLM backbones before costly fine-tuning or closed-loop evaluation. It also helps diagnose how model and training choices relate to embodied performance.

Limitations. These findings are correlations across a limited set of model families, benchmarks, and training recipes, with few models per comparison and one real-robot setting. Models with similar S² can still differ in task success, so closed-loop evaluation remains the final step in model selection. Whether training directly for higher S² improves robot task success remains an open question.
